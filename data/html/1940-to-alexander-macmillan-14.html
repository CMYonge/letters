My dear [[person:939]Mr Macmillan], I suppose [[cmybook:53]the Trial] ought to be stereotyped that it may stand on the same footing with the other books. In all the former cases I have been at the whole expense of printing, paper, binding &c, and have thus had [[other:160]all the profits], except the commission on the sales - I think the arrangement with regard to the Trial was that I was to receive £200 for 2,000 copies; I conclude therefore that the £68 would be separately my purchase of the stereotype plates - It makes a considerable diminution of the profits of the first edition, but it is certainly a necessary one and I agree to the arrangement.

I am glad you decided against [[other:167]illustrations] for the [[cmybook:20]Little Duke]. [[person:159]Mrs Blackburn]’s were not very successful, though she had been so kind about them that I should hardly have liked to put out others by a different hand without first giving her the option. Those to the [[cmybook:23]Lances of Lynwood] are much better, but she is never really at home without animals as her subject.

I think I prefer [[cmybook:51]Golden Deeds] to noble deeds, but that may be only because I have thought of the incidents by the former title, and besides it is a little more uncommon and vague - which last is a gain in a miscellaneous sort of collection, and gives a certain sense of freedom in gathering them. I have a delightful letter about them from [[person:941]Mrs Macmillan] which I am going to answer at once.

There are some grand incidents in the [[other:134]Indian Mutiny] such as [[person:1904]Lieutenant Willoughby]’s destruction of the powder at Delhi, Dr Hay remaining with the sick at Benares, and a young officer’s escape carrying a little girl of 3 years old.[[footnote:2]] But I never saw them but in the Times, and I suppose the only chance of getting the sight of them again would be in some library when I am in London again.

Yours faithfully C. M. Yonge
